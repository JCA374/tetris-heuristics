Data-driven learning, documented

From 1,785 to 413,000 lines by looking at how the bot dies

A heuristic Tetris bot with weights tuned by a genetic algorithm. The breakthrough did not come from more training or smarter rules but from recording thousands of lost games, measuring what predicts death, and adding the two features that were missing.

1 · The bot

Every move is a weighted sum

For each piece the bot tries every rotation and column, simulates the board after the drop, and scores it: score = Σ weight × feature. The highest score wins. A genetic algorithm searches for the weights. A regime model has several weight sets and picks one from the board before the move (e.g. "repair" when there are many holes).

2 · Measuring honestly

One game says almost nothing

The old genetic algorithm reported a best model of 5,968 lines. Replayed on fresh games, that model averages 1,785. It was the luckiest of thousands of noisy 5-game scores, a textbook winner's curse. Since then every number on this page comes from paired comparisons on held-out seeds that training never saw.

4 · Discovery

Recording 500 deaths found the missing features

We recorded 500 games of the original model: the final board, the last 300 boards before death and snapshots along the way. Then we computed 12 candidate features on every board and asked two questions: does a feature warn of death, and does it add information the model's own features do not already have?

How the original model dies

Final boards of 8 random games, sorted by lines cleared. Colors show the piece that filled each cell.

What a candidate adds beyond height, holes and bumpiness

Partial correlation with dying sooner after removing what the model's features already explain. The two highlighted became new features.

Deaths wait for an I-piece

Pieces since the last I-piece at death (bars) vs. what chance predicts (line). Long droughts are far over-represented: the board had become dependent on deep wells only an I can fill.

Holes pile up at the walls

Share of all holes at death by column. Dashed: an even spread (10%).

Result: two new features. wells (sum of d(d+1)/2 over columns lower than both neighbours, walls count as high) and hole depth (filled cells above each hole). Retrained with them, the bot went from ~2,500 to over 400,000 lines per game.

Change in mean lines when one feature is switched off (95% interval, paired on 50 games). Holes, bumpiness and wells are essential; height matters; hole depth is a tie-breaker.

Does the lines feature help?

Same idea with the earlier models: dropping the reward for clearing lines. With only 4 features it mattered; once wells and hole depth were in, the GA had set it to ~0 by itself, so v4 drops it.

6 · Regimes

Switching weight sets has not paid off (yet)

The original question: does a model that changes its weights depending on the situation beat one weight set? The 3-regime model (repair when holes ≥ 7, danger when height ≥ 15, else normal) lost clearly to the single model. The GA put the danger trigger so high it never fires, and repair only kicks in when the game is already lost.

8 · Training method

Measuring the rare event through the common ones

A strong model dies about once per million pieces, so a full game is one expensive, noisy data point. But deaths follow a ladder: the board gets a few holes every few hundred pieces, and only rarely climbs all the way up. Multilevel splitting measures each rung separately from short episodes, starting at clean boards and branching fresh continuations at 6, 10, 14 and 18 holes, and multiplies the chances. The crisis boards come from the evaluated model's own play, so there is no fixed set of crisis boards to overfit.

The ladder to game over (single_v4)

Pieces until the board first reaches N holes, from a clean board (log scale), and the chance of dying from there. Each rung is common; only the product is rare.

Does the estimate agree with full games?

Splitting estimate (median of 12, ~4 CPU-seconds each) vs. the full-game mean (50 held-out games), log-log. Same order and similar gaps; weak models are underestimated about 2×. One estimate is as informative as ~8 full games.

Optimizer test: GA vs cross-entropy, both from single_v4

Conclusion: neither optimizer beats single_v4 on full games. The 5-feature linear model is as good as it gets, so the next gains have to come from new information: move features and the next-piece preview.
9 · Next

What the data suggests next

  1. Move features. Landing height and eroded cells (Dellacherie). They describe the move, not the board, so they can target the 93% of non-S/Z hole moves where a hole-free option existed.
  2. Lookahead. Use the next-piece preview, which targets the forced S/Z holes.
  3. Train both with splitting fitness and confirm on 1,000+ full games. Regime switching is concluded: it never beat one weight set.
The method behind every chart here is written up in docs/ANALYSIS_METHOD.md: record failures with context, compute candidate signals, measure warning power and added information, then confirm with a paired experiment.
