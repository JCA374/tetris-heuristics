Data-driven learning, documented

From 1,785 to over 2.5 million lines by looking at how the bot dies

A heuristic Tetris bot with weights tuned by a genetic algorithm. The breakthrough did not come from more training or smarter rules but from recording thousands of lost games, measuring what predicts death, and adding the features that were missing. That worked twice: two board features took it to ~500,000 lines, three move features then to over 2.5 million.

1 · The bot

Every move is a weighted sum

For each piece the bot tries every rotation and column, simulates the board after the drop, and scores it: score = Σ weight × feature. The highest score wins. A genetic algorithm searches for the weights. Board features describe the board after the drop; move features (landing height, eroded cells, S/Z fit) describe the drop itself. A regime model (concluded research) has several weight sets and picks one from the board before the move.

2 · Measuring honestly

One game says almost nothing

The old genetic algorithm reported a best model of 5,968 lines. Replayed on fresh games, that model averages 1,785. It was the luckiest of thousands of noisy 5-game scores, a textbook winner's curse. Since then every number on this page comes from paired comparisons on held-out seeds that training never saw.

4 · Discovery

Recording 500 deaths found the missing features

We recorded 500 games of the original model: the final board, the last 300 boards before death and snapshots along the way. Then we computed 12 candidate features on every board and asked two questions: does a feature warn of death, and does it add information the model's own features do not already have?

Result: two new features. wells (sum of d(d+1)/2 over columns lower than both neighbours, walls count as high) and hole depth (filled cells above each hole). Retrained with them, the bot went from ~2,500 to over 400,000 lines per game.

6 · Regimes

Switching weight sets has not paid off (yet)

The original question: does a model that changes its weights depending on the situation beat one weight set? The 3-regime model (repair when holes ≥ 7, danger when height ≥ 15, else normal) lost clearly to the single model. The GA put the danger trigger so high it never fires, and repair only kicks in when the game is already lost.

8 · Move features

Three move features: about 5× more lines

The single_v4 analysis said the board features were used up, but it also showed where the holes come from: S and Z pieces make a hole in 12% of their moves (other pieces ~5%), and 93% of the hole moves by other pieces had a hole-free option the weights did not prefer. Both point at information the board after the drop does not carry, so three features describe the move itself:

  • landing height: where the piece lands (rows below it + half its own height). Place low.
  • eroded cells: rows the move clears × the piece's own cells in them. Clear with the piece.
  • S/Z fit: how many of S and Z (0–2) still have a hole-free spot after the move. Keep a home for S/Z.

single_v7 was trained from single_v4 with all eight features, using the same GA and splitting fitness that could not improve single_v4 on its own (section 9).

9 · Training method

Measuring the rare event through the common ones

The problem. A strong model dies about once per million pieces. One full game costs ~10 CPU-seconds and says little, because piece luck swings the result as much as the mean itself. A GA that needs many deaths per individual would take forever on full games.

The idea: split the rare event into common steps. The bot never dies out of nowhere. It climbs a ladder of trouble, and at every rung it either recovers to a clean board or climbs on:

clean board → 6 holes → 10 holes → 14 holes → 18 holes → game over

One estimate, step by step (illustrative numbers, roughly single_v4):

  1. Time to first trouble. Start from clean boards taken from real games and play until the board has 6 holes: on average ~3,000 pieces.
  2. Split at every rung. From each 6-hole board, play 8 continuations with different random pieces and count how many reach 10 holes before recovering: say 10%. Repeat from the 10-hole boards to 14 (30%), 14 to 18 (40%) and 18 to game over (20%).
  3. Multiply. P(trouble ends in death) = 0.10 × 0.30 × 0.40 × 0.20 = 0.0024, so expected pieces per death ≈ 3,000 ÷ 0.0024 ≈ 1.25 million. Its logarithm is the training fitness; charts convert it to estimated lines per game (≈ pieces ÷ 2.5).

Why it is better. One estimate takes ~4 CPU-seconds and carries as much information as ~8 full games (~70 CPU-seconds): about 17× more information per second. Compute goes to the dangerous moments, not the millions of easy pieces in between. The trouble boards come from the evaluated model's own play, so there is no fixed crisis dataset to memorise (training on recorded crisis boards did overfit: 76% recovery on its own boards, 71% on new ones).

Caveats. It is an estimate: for single_v4 it says ~1.45M pieces per death vs ~1.2M in real games, a bit high but in the right order. Borderline wins can be false positives (an 18% splitting win below was 0% on 1,000 full games), and during training the GA keeps the individuals with lucky estimates, so training curves run high. Splitting trains and screens; full games decide.

Does the estimate agree with full games?

Splitting estimate (median of 12, ~4 CPU-seconds each) vs. the full-game mean (50 held-out games), log-log. Same order and similar gaps; weak models are underestimated about 2×. One estimate is as informative as ~8 full games.

Conclusion: neither optimizer beats single_v4 on full games. The 5-feature linear model is as good as it gets, so the next gains have to come from new information: move features and the next-piece preview.
